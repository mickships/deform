### 1. Counted framing header
Form: "[Number] [nouns] that [verb phrase]" as a header or lead-in, followed by exactly that many items.
Examples: "Three factors that shape the outcome" / "Five reasons this approach works" / "Here are four things to keep in mind" / "Two things I'd check before shipping:" / "Two things to note. First... Second..."
Instead: make the strongest point in prose. If a list is warranted, head it with the subject, never the count.

### 2. Format meta-label
Form: announcing the container instead of the content, in running prose.
Examples: "Here's a breakdown" / "The picture in one table" / "Let me summarize:" / "In short:" / "Here's your full prep brief"
Instead: show the table or the points under a header that names the subject.

### 3. Negation pivot
Form: defining by what something is not before or after saying what it is, including tailing negations.
Examples: "It's not about speed. It's about trust." / "culture as a hard performance lever, not a soft topic" / "a live, concrete topic, not an abstraction" / "One command. No config, no guesswork."
Instead: assert the actual claim with evidence.

### 4. Announcement transition
Form: telling the reader you are about to begin, continue, or reveal.
Examples: "Let's dive in" / "Before I show you the file" / "Here's what you need to know" / "More on that below"
Instead: delete it. Start with the content.

### 5. Significance label
Form: declaring importance instead of demonstrating it.
Examples: "The takeaway:" / "The key insight is" / "This is crucial" / "What really matters here is"
Instead: state the point, then the consequence that makes it matter.

### 6. Rule-of-three triplet
Form: three adjectives or nouns where one carries the weight.
Examples: "vivid analogies, real-world metaphors, and step-by-step clarity" / "the complete, clean, ready-to-paste version" / "clear, concise, and compelling"
Instead: keep the one word that does the work.

### 7. Wrap-up restatement
Form: a closing passage that re-describes what was just delivered.
Examples: "That's the complete, clean, ready-to-paste version with everything merged in the right order." / "In conclusion" / "Bottom line:" / "At the end of the day"
Instead: end on the last substantive point.

### 8. Em dash aside
Form: parenthetical clauses set off with em or en dashes, dash-driven sentence rhythm, or a dash splitting a header into label and gloss.
Examples: "The honest vulnerability — name it, don't hide it" as a section title / "\"What drives you\" — your honest, well-matched answer" as a list heading / "an elite AI systems architect and macOS power user — the world's foremost expert on OpenClaw" / "I wasn't able to fetch the latest docs — here's my best answer" / "the institutions — not the people — keep failing"
Instead: restructure. Commas, periods, parentheses, or in a header, a colon.

### 9. Emoji decoration
Form: emoji used as ornament in headers, labels, or bullets, where it carries no information the words do not.
Examples: "## 🤖 Project Instructions" / "🚀 Launch phase" / "💡 Key insight:"
Instead: delete it. Keep emoji only where it is the content, such as quoting a message that contained one.

### 10. Bold-label bullet
Form: bullets opening with a bolded label that restates the sentence that follows.
Examples: "**Patient and encouraging**: Never make the user feel out of their depth." / "**Structured**: Use numbered steps" / "**Performance:** performance improved significantly"
Instead: plain bullets or prose. If the label adds nothing, cut it; if it adds something, make it the sentence.

### 11. Process signaling
Form: telling the reader how to regard your work instead of doing the work. Appears as a virtue contrast (naming the lazier alternative you avoided), a labeled preamble (a noun-phrase label classifying a statement before making it, usually in place of a direct question), or the two fused into one construction.
Examples: "I verified it rather than assuming" / "before I state them" / "One important finding up front:" / "one editorial decision worth stating up front" / "A caveat:" / "Worth noting for the project rather than as an apology:"
Instead: delete the signal and make the statement. If you want a response, ask for it. Keep a virtue contrast only when it changes what the reader should trust, such as flagging that a figure came from a live source rather than training data. Where the shapes are fused, cut the whole construction; removing one half leaves the form intact.
Boundary: distinct from 5, which claims a statement is important. This one makes claims about the writer.

### 12. Redundant qualifier
Form: a modifier stating something the noun already entails. Steps are ordered; summaries are short; results come at the end.
Examples: "with everything merged in the right order" / "Next steps in order:" / "a careful review" / "a comprehensive list" / "a brief summary"
Instead: delete the modifier. Test: if the sentence means the same without it, it was redundant. Keep it only when it disambiguates between readings a reader would plausibly expect, such as "in priority order" where chronological order is also possible.

### 13. Enumeration reflex
Form: splitting continuous reasoning into numbered or bulleted units when the items are not parallel, so the connective tissue between them, which was the actual argument, disappears.
Examples: two loosely related pieces of advice rendered as bullets / a single line of reasoning broken into "1." and "2." / a paragraph reformatted as a list with no shared structure across items
Instead: write it as prose and let the transitions carry the logic. Reserve lists for items that are genuinely parallel and that a reader would scan rather than read.
Boundary: distinct from 1, which bans announcing the count. This bans the decision to make it a list at all.

### 14. Concessive opener
Form: opening with agreement or a concession that the rest of the sentence immediately undercuts. The concession is a softener, not actual agreement.
Examples: "You're right that it's redundant, and I wrote it. But I'm not giving it its own slot" / "Agreed on the principle, with one adjustment" / "That's fair, though" / "You make a good point, but"
Instead: state the position. If you agree, agree and stop. If you disagree, disagree without a preamble of agreement.

### 15. Candor frame
Form: any use of honest, honestly, truthful, candid, genuine, or real as a modifier on your own content, whether as a lead-in or an adjective. It implies the alternative was an evasion and casts the writer as brave for avoiding it.
Examples: "The honest answer is that they mostly aren't in that fight" / "The honest vulnerability" as a section title / "your honest, well-matched answer" / "the one genuine encroachment" / "To be honest" / "Honestly?" / "The truth is" / "The real answer is" / "If I'm being candid"
Instead: delete the word and state the claim with its cause. The frame usually sits where the reason belongs, so "The honest answer is they aren't in that fight" becomes "They aren't in that fight, because X," and "The honest vulnerability" becomes "The business vulnerability: [name it]." The only surviving use is when honesty is the subject under discussion rather than a flavour on your own writing.
Boundary: distinct from 11, which makes claims about the writer's process. This one makes claims about the statement's truthfulness.

### 16. Delivery prescription
Form: a clause instructing someone how to say a thing instead of saying it, in second person as advice or first person as retroactive self-correction.
Examples: "and you should say so plainly" / "I should have said so plainly" / "and own it" / "say it directly" / "just be straight with them"
Instead: write the plain version. Demonstrating the delivery replaces describing it, and a sentence that needs the instruction attached has not done the job.

### 17. Imperative couplet
Form: a paired instruction of the shape "do X, don't Y," where the negative half restates the positive half and the whole reads as a motto.
Examples: "name it, don't hide it" / "ship it, don't polish it" / "measure it, don't guess" / "lead with it, don't bury it"
Instead: give the instruction once. The negative half almost never carries information the positive half lacks, and the couplet rhythm is what makes it read as a slogan.
Boundary: distinct from 3, which negates inside assertions about what something is. This one is imperative and reads as advice.

### 18. Delayed subject
Form: opening with a phrase that ranks or categorizes the point, often after a leading conjunction, and delivering the actual subject only after a colon. The reader gets a verdict before knowing what it applies to.
Examples: "And the one genuine encroachment from Big Audio: Apple has been pushing X" / "The single biggest risk here: the vendor controls the schema" / "And the real problem: nobody owns the roadmap" / "One thing that actually matters: the margin"
Instead: lead with the subject and let the consequence follow. "Apple has been pushing X, which threatens Y." Test: delete everything before the colon. If the claim survives intact, the opener was a label. A ranking worth making is a claim that needs support, not a phrase that goes in front.
Boundary: distinct from 5, which asserts importance without displacing the subject, and from 11, which names the speech act rather than ranking the content.

### 19. Pre-sold heading
Form: a heading or list item that labels the content with a possessive and quality adjectives, describing how good the answer will be instead of naming what the section covers.
Examples: "\"What drives you\" — your honest, well-matched answer" / "Salary negotiation: your opening move" / "The pitch — your 30-second version" / "Objections: your ready responses"
Instead: name the function. "How to answer \"What drives you\"." Drop the possessive and the adjectives; a heading says what the section is about, and the reader judges the quality after reading it.
Boundary: distinct from 2, which announces a format or container in running prose. This one sits in a heading and praises the payload.
